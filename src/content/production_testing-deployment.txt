Testing and deployment
7.4.1
Zero-downtime deployment
#

Rolling updates assume replicas start quickly. Inference replicas do not, so a naive rolling deploy removes capacity minutes before it adds it back.

Bring the new version fully up, verify it serves real requests, shift traffic, then tear down the old version. That means paying for both fleets during the overlap, and that cost is the price of not taking an outage on every deploy. Canary a small share of traffic first — quality regressions from a new model or a new quantization show up in outputs, not in error rates.

7.4.2
Cost estimation
#

Cost per token is the number the business cares about, and it falls out of three things you already know: GPU hourly rate, achieved throughput, and utilization.

Utilization is the term people omit, and it is the one that dominates. A GPU busy 30% of the day costs three times per token what the same GPU busy 90% of the day does, with identical hardware and identical optimization. Most inference cost problems are utilization problems wearing a performance costume — which is the same point the crossover calculator in Chapter 1 makes.

7.4.3
Observability
#

Instrument the numbers from Chapter 1 at percentiles, not averages: TTFT, inter-token latency, total response time, queue depth, batch size, cache hit rate, GPU utilization, and cost per token.

Two things separate useful inference observability from generic service monitoring. Measure end-to-end and on-GPU separately — the gap between them tells you whether to look at the model or the infrastructure. And monitor output quality, not just latency: a model that degrades after a config change produces perfectly fast, perfectly available, subtly worse answers, and nothing in a standard dashboard will tell you.

The alert that matters

Queue depth. It rises before latency does, which makes it the earliest honest signal that capacity is short. By the time p99 moves, users have already had the bad experience.
